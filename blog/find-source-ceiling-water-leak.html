How to Find the Source of a Ceiling Water Leak and Fix It

The water rarely enters right above the stain. Here is how to trace a ceiling leak back to its real source — and stop it for good.

Updated November 2026 · 10 min read · By the Ceiling Water Damage Repair Phoenix AZ team

Here is the frustrating truth about ceiling leaks: the water almost never enters directly above the stain you see. It travels. Water finds the path of least resistance, running along the top of the drywall, following joists and pipes, and dripping through at the lowest point it can reach — sometimes several feet from where it actually got in. That is why so many ceiling repairs fail: the stain gets patched, but the real entry point, hidden off to one side, keeps letting water through. Finding the true source is the difference between a repair that lasts and one that stains again after the next storm.

This guide is a practical troubleshooting walkthrough for Phoenix homes. When the trail goes cold — and sometimes it does — our Phoenix ceiling water damage repair team uses moisture mapping and thermal imaging to see the water you cannot. But many sources can be tracked down with patience and a flashlight, so let us start there.

The most common sources in Phoenix homes

  • Roof and monsoon leaks — worn or lifted flashing, cracked or slipped tiles, and wind-driven summer rain
  • AC condensate lines — clogged drain lines and overflowing drain pans from attic air handlers
  • Plumbing leaks — supply lines, drain lines and fittings running above the ceiling
  • Water heater failures — especially units installed in attics or upstairs closets
  • Roof penetrations — vents, skylights and solar mounts where seals fail over time

Step 1: Use the timing of the leak as a clue

When the leak appears tells you a great deal about where it is coming from. A stain that worsens only during or right after storms points strongly to the roof. One that appears during the hot cooling season, with no rain in sight, often traces to an AC condensate line overflowing. A leak that is constant, or unrelated to weather and temperature, usually means plumbing. Before you climb into the attic, ask yourself: when does this get worse?

Step 2: Look directly above — then uphill

From the attic (take a flashlight and step only on the joists), start above the stain and trace the framing. Because water runs downhill along the structure, you are looking for the wet trail and following it "uphill" to its highest wet point, which is usually near the entry. Look for water tracks and staining on the underside of the roof deck, rust on nails or fasteners, damp or compressed insulation, and discoloration on the framing. The highest point where things are wet is your best clue to the source.

Step 3: Check the usual suspects

The AC condensate system

This is one of the most common and most overlooked causes in the Valley. Attic air handlers produce condensation that drains through a line; when that line clogs, water backs up and overflows the drain pan onto the ceiling below. Check the drain pan and line for standing water, clogs or rust. A clogged condensate line is often an easy fix once identified.

The roof

Inspect flashing around vents, valleys and the chimney, look for cracked or slipped tiles, and check the seals around skylights and penetrations. Monsoon-driven rain exploits any gap. Roof sources often require sealing from above.

Plumbing

Any supply or drain line running above the ceiling — for an upstairs bathroom, a laundry room, or a second-floor fixture — can leak at a fitting or develop a pinhole. A constant leak unrelated to weather points here.

Step 4: Fix the source, then restore the ceiling

Finding the source is only half the job — it has to be fixed before the interior is restored, or the new ceiling simply gets wet again. For roof-related leaks, the entry point must be sealed from above. Our roof leak repair service traces the source and coordinates the fix so the ceiling you just restored does not re-stain in the next storm. Once the source is resolved, the wet cavity still needs proper drying; our guide on drying a water-damaged ceiling before repairs explains how to dry the structure before any rebuild.

What to do when you can't find the source

Sometimes the trail genuinely goes cold — the attic is inaccessible, the leak is intermittent, or the water is traveling a long, hidden path. This is where professional tools earn their keep. Thermal imaging cameras reveal the cool signature of moisture behind ceilings and walls, and moisture meters map exactly how far the water has spread. A coordinated water damage restoration approach brings those tools together to find the source, dry the structure and rebuild the ceiling as one managed process, rather than leaving you to chase a leak you cannot see.

Preventing the next ceiling leak

  • Have the roof inspected and serviced before monsoon season each year
  • Keep AC condensate lines clear — a simple seasonal flush prevents clogs
  • Check the seals around skylights, vents and penetrations periodically
  • Address small stains and drips promptly, before they become big ones
  • Know where your water shutoff is, so a plumbing failure can be stopped fast

Tools that make leak tracing easier

You can get a long way with a flashlight and patience, but a few inexpensive tools help. A bright headlamp frees your hands in the attic. A simple moisture meter — available at any hardware store — lets you compare damp areas against dry ones and follow the trail of moisture toward its source. Chalk or a pencil to mark wet spots and the edge of a stain helps you see whether things are spreading. And a hose, used carefully with a helper watching inside, can sometimes confirm a suspected roof entry point by recreating the conditions that cause the leak. Professionals add thermal imaging cameras, which reveal the temperature difference of hidden moisture, but the basics will solve many household leaks.

A quick room-by-room guide to likely sources

  • Top-floor rooms and hallways: roof leaks and attic condensate lines are the usual suspects
  • Ceilings below a bathroom: shower pans, toilet seals, tub overflows and supply lines
  • Ceilings below a laundry room: washer supply hoses and drain connections
  • Ceilings near an attic air handler: condensate drain pan overflow is extremely common in Phoenix
  • Ceilings below a water heater: tank or fitting failure, especially for attic or closet units

Why leaks can be so hard to trace

Even experienced homeowners sometimes cannot pin down a ceiling leak, and there are good reasons. Water travels along framing and can surface far from its entry point. Intermittent leaks — a roof that only leaks under wind-driven rain, or a pipe that only drips under certain pressure — are quiet most of the time. Modern homes have insulation, vapor barriers and tight cavities that hide the trail. And some entry points, like a hairline crack in flashing or a pinhole in a pipe, are tiny despite causing real damage. When you have checked the obvious sources and still cannot find it, that is exactly the point where professional moisture mapping and thermal imaging save time and prevent repeated, failed repairs.

Frequently asked questions

Why is the leak showing up far from where I think the water enters?

Because water runs along the top of the ceiling and down the framing before dripping through at the lowest point. The stain marks where it exits, not where it enters — which is why tracing it "uphill" matters.

My ceiling only leaks when it rains. What does that mean?

That points to a roof or flashing issue that only admits water under wind-driven rain. The ceiling damage still needs drying and repair, and the entry point needs sealing before the next monsoon.

Can a clogged AC line really cause a ceiling leak?

Absolutely — it is one of the most common causes in Phoenix. An attic air handler's condensate line clogs, water overflows the drain pan, and it drips onto the ceiling below, often during the hottest part of the year.

The bottom line

A ceiling leak is a detective problem before it is a repair problem. Resist the urge to patch the stain until you have traced the water back to where it truly enters, because fixing the symptom while ignoring the source guarantees you will be back with a roller before long. Use the timing of the leak, follow the moisture uphill in the attic, and check the usual Phoenix culprits — roof flashing, AC condensate lines and plumbing above the ceiling. Fix the source, dry the cavity, then restore the ceiling, and it will stay clean through the next monsoon and the next cooling season alike.

Key Takeaways

  • The stain marks where water exits, not where it enters — trace it uphill.
  • Use the timing of the leak (storms vs. heat vs. constant) to narrow the source.
  • Clogged AC condensate lines are among the most common Phoenix culprits.
  • Fix the source before restoring the ceiling, or it will re-stain.
  • When the source is hidden, thermal imaging and moisture mapping find it.
